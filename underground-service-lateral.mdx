## Underground service lateral in Clearwater

Underground Service Lateral, called Buried Service Cable, is a type of Electrical Service Conductor that runs from the utility transformer to the meter base entirely below ground, carrying the full supply voltage through conduit, insulated conductors, tracer wire and a protective sheath buried at a regulated depth.

### Parts and scope of the underground service lateral

Underground Service Lateral is made up of several distinct components that each carry a specific role in delivering power safely from the street to the structure. The conduit, typically PVC, HDPE or rigid steel, forms the outer channel and shields the conductors from soil movement, moisture and mechanical damage. Inside the conduit, insulated copper or aluminum conductors carry the current, sized in AWG or kcmil depending on the load demand of the building. Conduit elbows redirect the run at the transformer end and at the meter base entry point, and tracer wire runs alongside the conduit so the buried path can be located without excavation. A protective jacket over the conductors and insulation guards against abrasion and chemical exposure in the soil. See [electrician](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/electrician).

### Measurable attributes of a service lateral

Underground Service Lateral carries a defined set of measurable attributes that determine whether an installation meets code for a given property. Conduit diameter ranges from 1.5 to 4 inches, selected based on conductor count and size. Burial depth requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. Conductor material is either copper or aluminum, and conductor gauge is expressed in AWG for smaller runs or kcmil for larger commercial feeds. Voltage capacity covers standard residential supply up to 240 V and extends into higher kV ratings for commercial and industrial feeds. Current capacity requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. Run length, measured in meters, affects voltage drop calculations, which in turn influence the minimum conductor gauge required for the installation.

### How the underground service lateral connects to the broader electrical system

Underground Service Lateral connects directly to the electric meter at one end and to the utility transformer at the other, making it the first segment of the building's electrical supply path. It is a component of the [service entrance](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/service-entrance), the broader assembly that includes the meter base, service entrance conductors and the main disconnect. From the meter, power moves into the main service panel, where branch circuits distribute it through the building. The Buried Service Line interfaces with [grounding systems](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/grounding-systems) at the service entrance, where the grounding electrode conductor bonds the neutral to earth ground. Utility coordination requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority.

### Underground service lateral vs. Overhead service drop

Underground Service Lateral stands in direct contrast to an overhead service drop. An overhead service drop delivers utility power through aerial conductors strung from a pole to a weatherhead on the building exterior. Aerial conductors run above grade, leaving them exposed to wind, ice, falling trees, and UV degradation. Buried Service Cable avoids those hazards but faces soil movement, root intrusion, and groundwater instead. Interior wiring is a separate category entirely. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. Underground installation adds excavation cost and requires precise burial depth compliance, yet it eliminates the visual impact of overhead lines and reduces storm-related outages.

### Common problems and signs of a failing buried service cable

Underground Service Lateral failures are recognizable. They most often appear as intermittent power loss, low voltage at outlets, tripped main breakers with no clear cause, or a burning smell near the meter base. Conductor insulation degrades over decades in wet soil. Aluminum conductors are particularly susceptible to oxidation at connection points, which raises resistance and generates heat. Conduit joints can separate from soil settlement, allowing water to pool around the conductors. Physical damage from excavation, whether from landscaping, irrigation work, or utility trenching, can crush or sever the conduit and conductors without any immediate visible sign at the meter. A voltage reading below the expected supply level at the meter base, while the utility confirms full voltage at the transformer, points to resistance or a break inside the Buried Service Line.
