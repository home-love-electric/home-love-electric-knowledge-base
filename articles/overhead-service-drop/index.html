Overhead Service Drop: A Home Love Electric Reference Guide

Updated November 2026

Overhead service drop in Clearwater

Overhead Service Drop, called an Overhead Electrical Drop, is the set of utility-owned conductors that run from the utility pole to a building's weatherhead, carrying 120/240V electricity across the span between the street infrastructure and the structure's service entrance. The aerial service drop connects directly to the electric meter, feeds the main service panel, and forms the first link in the building's electrical supply chain.

Overhead service drop components and scope

Overhead Service Drop is a type of Electrical Service Entrance, the broader category that includes every method of bringing utility power into a structure. The service drop itself spans from the last utility pole to the attachment point on the building, and its component parts are the phase conductors, the neutral conductor, the messenger wire, the weatherhead, the service bracket, support brackets and insulators. Phase conductors carry the live current; the neutral conductor completes the circuit and bonds to the grounding system at the panel. The weatherhead is the curved conduit fitting at the upper end of the service entrance mast that keeps rain out of the conduit. Service entrance components pick up where the utility's drop ends, carrying power from the weatherhead down to the meter and into the panel.

Measurable attributes of a service drop

Overhead Service Drop carries standard residential voltage of 120/240V, delivered as two 120V legs and a neutral. Conductor size ranges from 4 AWG on older or lighter-load services up to 2/0 AWG on larger residential feeds, with conductor material being either aluminum or copper. Aluminum is the dominant choice for utility-side drops because of its lighter weight over long spans. Amperage capacity runs from 100A on older homes to 200A or 400A on upgraded services. Attachment height requirements at the building can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. The length of the span from pole to weatherhead affects conductor sag, and longer runs require a larger conductor gauge to keep voltage drop within acceptable limits. Main service panel upgrade projects often require the utility to evaluate whether the existing service drop conductor size supports the new amperage before the upgrade proceeds.

How the overhead service drop connects to the broader electrical system

Overhead Service Drop connects to the electric meter at the bottom of the service entrance conductors, and the meter feeds the main service panel inside the building. The utility installs and owns the service drop up to the weatherhead attachment point. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority.

Overhead service drop requirements and installation

Conductor clearance requirements over rooftops, driveways, sidewalks, and public roads can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority.

Overhead service drop vs. Underground service lateral

Overhead Service Drop is the aerial alternative to an Underground Service Lateral, which routes conductors through conduit buried below grade from the utility transformer to the meter base. The underground service lateral eliminates exposure to wind, falling trees and physical damage from vehicles, but it costs more to install and is harder to inspect or repair after burial. Aerial service drops are more common in established neighborhoods where underground conversion requires trenching through finished landscaping and driveways. Either method terminates at the same meter base and feeds the same panel, so the panel-side electrical work is identical regardless of which service entrance method the utility uses. Underground service lateral projects follow the same inspection sequence once the conductors reach the meter base.

Common problems with an overhead service drop

Overhead Service Drop problems most often show up as flickering lights on one leg of the service, a burning smell near the weatherhead, visible fraying or insulation damage on the conductors, or a meter base that runs hot to the touch. Conductor insulation degrades over decades of UV exposure, and aluminum conductors can develop oxidation at connection points that raises resistance and generates heat. See service entrance.
