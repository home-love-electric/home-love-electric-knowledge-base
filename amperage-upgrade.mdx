## Amperage upgrade in Clearwater

An amperage upgrade, called a Current Capacity Increase, is replacement and expansion of an electrical system's current-carrying capacity by swapping out the existing service panel, service cables and main switch for components rated at a higher amperage, typically moving a home from a 100-amp original rating to a 200-amp new rating so the system can safely power modern appliances, EV chargers, HVAC equipment and additional circuits without tripping breakers or violating code.

### What an amperage upgrade includes

An amperage upgrade is a type of Electrical Service Upgrade, the broader category of work that brings an electrical system's infrastructure in line with current load demands and safety standards. The upgrade covers three physical component parts: the circuit breakers, the service cables running from the utility meter to the panel, and the main switch that controls power to the entire system. Replacing all three together raises the system's rated capacity rather than patching one weak point. A [home electrical capacity increase](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/home-electrical-capacity-increase) of this kind adds available breaker spaces, commonly expanding from a crowded 12-space panel to a 30-space panel, which leaves room for future circuits without another full upgrade.

### Measurable attributes of a panel amperage increase

The Original Amperage Rating is the capacity the existing panel was built for, most often 60 amps in older homes built before the 1970s or 100 amps in homes built through the 1990s. The New Amperage Rating is the target capacity after the upgrade, with 200 amps being the standard for a residential amperage upgrade today and 400 amps available for larger homes or those with high-draw equipment. Available Breaker Spaces rise alongside amperage, giving the panel room to support dedicated circuits for kitchen appliances, home offices and EV charging stations. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. A [200 amp service upgrade](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/200-amp-service-upgrade) is the standard target rating for single-family homes in Clearwater.

### How an amperage upgrade relates to the broader electrical system

An amperage upgrade enables increased power consumption across every branch circuit in the home because the service entrance is the single point that limits how much current the entire system can draw at once. Without a higher-rated service entrance, adding circuits downstream does not solve the underlying capacity problem. The upgrade is performed on the electrical panel, which is the distribution hub connecting the utility feed to every outlet, switch, fixture and appliance in the building. A completed upgrade often incorporates a [main service panel](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/main-service-panel) rated for the new capacity along with proper [load balancing](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/load-balancing-electrical). ### Signs of insufficient electrical capacity

Insufficient Electrical Capacity is the condition an amperage upgrade corrects. It appears in several measurable ways before a panel fails outright. Frequent breaker trips are the most common sign. A breaker that trips repeatedly under normal household loads is reaching the panel's rated limit, not a single circuit's limit. Lights dim when a large appliance starts. Outlets that feel warm to the touch and a main breaker that runs hot are physical indicators that the existing service is operating near or above its safe threshold. Load limitation is the practical consequence: homeowners cannot add an EV charger, a hot tub, a second HVAC unit, or a whole-home generator circuit without first raising the service amperage, because the math of the new loads exceeds what a 100-amp panel can safely carry. ### Where a residential amperage upgrade applies

A residential amperage upgrade applies to any single-family home, townhouse, or small multifamily building whose original service rating no longer matches the electrical load the occupants actually run. Homes built before 1980 are common candidates. Construction standards of that era assumed fewer high-draw devices per household. Renovation projects that add a kitchen, a primary suite, a detached garage, or an accessory dwelling unit frequently require a panel amperage increase as part of the permit package, since each new space adds circuits that the original panel may not be able to absorb. New appliance installations are another trigger. Electric vehicle chargers, tankless water heaters, and whole-home standby generators each draw enough current that a 100-amp service cannot support them alongside existing loads.
