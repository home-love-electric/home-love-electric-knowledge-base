## Subpanel in Clearwater

A subpanel, called a sub-distribution board, is a type of electrical panel that receives power from the main service panel and redistributes it through its own set of circuit breakers to a specific area, structure, or zone of a building. It does not generate or meter power on its own. Instead, it is a downstream distribution point, extending the electrical system into a garage, workshop, addition, dock, or any space too far from the main panel to wire directly without voltage drop or overcrowding.

### Parts that make up a subpanel

A subpanel contains four core components: a housing enclosure, bus bars, a main breaker or main lugs, and individual circuit breakers. The housing enclosure is a metal cabinet rated for indoor or outdoor installation depending on where it is mounted. Bus bars are the copper or aluminum bars inside the enclosure that carry current from the feeder cable to each breaker slot. Circuit breakers clip onto the bus bars and protect individual circuits from overload. Unlike the [main breaker panel](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/main-breaker-panel), a subpanel does not connect directly to the utility meter. It draws its power from a dedicated double-pole breaker in the main panel, which is the subpanel's shutoff.

### Measurable attributes of a subpanel

Subpanels carry specific, measurable ratings that determine what loads they can safely handle. Amperage is the primary rating. Residential subpanels typically run from 50 amps on the low end to 200 amps for large detached structures. Breaker spaces range from 12 to 42 slots depending on enclosure size, with larger panels used when a garage or addition requires many dedicated circuits. Voltage is 120/240V in standard residential installations, meaning the panel supports both single-pole 120V circuits and double-pole 240V circuits for appliances like air conditioners and water heaters. Load capacity is the total wattage the panel can distribute at once, calculated by multiplying amperage by voltage. A 100-amp, 240V subpanel carries a maximum load capacity of 24,000 watts. Circuit capacity is set by the number of breaker spaces and cannot exceed the enclosure's physical slot count. Many installations draw from an [amperage upgrade](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/amperage-upgrade) performed at the main service panel first.

### How a subpanel connects to the broader electrical distribution system

A subpanel is part of the electrical distribution system, sitting one level below the main service panel in the distribution hierarchy. Power flows from the utility transformer to the meter, then into the main service panel, then through a feeder cable to the subpanel. The feeder cable is sized to match the subpanel's amperage rating. A 60-amp subpanel needs a feeder sized for 60 amps, typically 6-gauge copper wire. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. Proper feeder selection often requires review of the existing [main service panel](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/main-service-panel) capacity.

### Subpanel vs. Main panel

A subpanel is not a main panel, and the distinction matters for both safety and code compliance. The [main service panel](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/main-service-panel) is the first point of distribution after the utility meter and carries the full service amperage. Commonly 150 or 200 amps in residential homes. A secondary panel, by contrast, receives only a portion of that capacity through a dedicated breaker and cannot exceed the amperage of the feeder protecting it. The main panel bonds the neutral and ground together at a single point; the subpanel keeps them separated. Installing a subpanel does not increase the home's total service capacity. If the main panel is already near its load limit, adding a remote panel without upgrading the service will overload the system. This is why load calculations come before any subpanel installation. A [load center](https://home-love-electric.mintlify.site/load-center) functions in the same role when used as a subpanel.

### Common reasons to install a subpanel

Installing a subpanel makes sense when a detached structure or high-demand zone needs more circuits than the main panel can spare. Some loads are simply too large. A detached garage with EV charging and power tools can easily require six to ten circuits, consuming most of the available slots in a standard main panel. Dock wiring, a service Home Love Electric provides, frequently requires a dedicated electrical subpanel because the distance from the main panel to the water's edge creates voltage drop on long runs. A remote panel placed closer to the dock solves both the drop and the circuit-count problem. Workshops and home offices with dedicated HVAC are other common applications, as are additions carrying their own sub-loads. A secondary panel simplifies future circuit additions, since new circuits tap into the subpanel locally rather than running long home-runs back to the main panel.

### Signs a subpanel needs attention

Subpanels show specific warning signs when they are undersized, aging, or improperly installed. Some signs are immediate. Breakers that trip repeatedly under normal loads indicate the panel's amperage rating is too low for the circuits it serves. Warm or hot spots on the enclosure surface point to a loose connection or an overloaded bus bar. Corrosion on bus bars or breaker terminals, common in coastal climates like Clearwater, increases resistance and generates heat. These conditions present real risks of fire and shock and should not be deferred.
