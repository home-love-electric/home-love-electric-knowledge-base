Glossary — Home Love Electric

Promotional and educational coverage of Home Love Electric. Not operated by Home Love Electric.

  • Main Breaker Panel: A main breaker panel, called a service panel or breaker box, is a type of electrical panel that receives incoming utility power and distributes it to every circuit in a home through individual circuit breakers, a main breaker switch, busbars and an enclosure that houses the entire assembly.
  • 200 Amp Service Upgrade: A 200 amp service upgrade, called a 200A Main Panel Upgrade, replaces and expands a property's main electrical entry point to support a 200-ampere capacity.
  • Amperage Upgrade: An amperage upgrade, called a Current Capacity Increase, is replacement and expansion of an electrical system's current-carrying capacity by swapping out the existing service panel, service cables and main switch for components rated at a higher amperage, typically moving a home from a 100-amp original rating to a 200-amp new rating so the system can safely power modern appliances, EV chargers, HVAC equipment and additional circuits without tripping breakers or violating code.
  • Fuse Box Replacement: Fuse box replacement, called Fuse to Breaker Conversion, is the removal of an outdated fuse box and the installation of a new circuit breaker panel, covering the disconnection of the old fuses, the mounting of a new enclosure, the wiring of branch circuits, the connection of the main breaker and bus bars and the restoration of power to every circuit in the home.
  • Subpanel: A subpanel, called a sub-distribution board, is a type of electrical panel that receives power from the main service panel and redistributes it through its own set of circuit breakers to a specific area, structure, or zone of a building.
  • Load Center: A load center, called a breaker panel or breaker box, is an electrical enclosure that receives incoming power from the utility and distributes it to individual circuits throughout a home through a set of circuit breakers, bus bars, a neutral bus and a metal enclosure.
  • Overhead Service Drop: Overhead Service Drop, called an Overhead Electrical Drop, is the set of utility-owned conductors that run from the utility pole to a building's weatherhead, carrying 120/240V electricity across the span between the street infrastructure and the structure's service entrance.
  • Underground Service Lateral: Underground Service Lateral, called Buried Service Cable, is a type of Electrical Service Conductor that runs from the utility transformer to the meter base entirely below ground, carrying the full supply voltage through conduit, insulated conductors, tracer wire and a protective sheath buried at a regulated depth.
  • Weatherhead: A weatherhead, called a service entrance cap or mast head, is a fitting that protects electrical service entrance conductors from rain, wind and debris at the point where utility lines connect to a building's electrical system.
  • Smart Meter: A smart meter, called an Advanced Metering Infrastructure meter or AMI meter, is a type of digital electric meter that records electricity consumption in kilowatt-hours, transmits usage data to the utility in real time over a two-way communication link, supports remote disconnect and reconnect commands, and replaces the older electromechanical spinning-disk meters that required a technician to read on-site.
  • Neutral Bus Bar: A neutral bus bar, called a neutral terminal strip, is a conductive metal bar mounted inside an electrical panel that collects the neutral return current from every circuit in the building and routes it back to the utility through the main service neutral conductor.
  • Bonding Jumper: A bonding jumper, called a bonding wire, is a type of electrical connector.
  • Main Disconnect: The main disconnect, called the main breaker or service disconnect, is the primary electrical switch for an entire building, controlling the flow of electricity from the utility line into the service panel and, from there, to every circuit in the structure.
  • Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter: A ground fault circuit interrupter, commonly called a GFCI, is a type of Circuit Protection Device that monitors the flow of electricity through a circuit.
  • Generator Interlock Kit: A generator interlock kit, called a generator lockout kit, is a mechanical electrical safety device that mounts directly onto a residential or commercial load center and physically prevents the main breaker and the generator breaker from being closed at the same time.
  • Main Service Panel: The main service panel, called an Electrical Panel or Breaker Panel, is the central distribution point where utility electricity enters a building and splits into individual branch circuits.
  • Electrical Utility Connection: Electrical utility connection, called a Utility Service Drop, is the physical point where a utility provider's power lines meet a building's electrical system, delivering voltage through service entrance conductors, a weatherhead, a meter base, connectors and insulation that together form the complete path from the street to the structure.
  • Insufficient Electrical Capacity: Insufficient electrical capacity, called an Electrical Overload or Inadequate Amperage, is a condition where a home's electrical system cannot safely supply the required amperage for its current or planned loads, covering the service entrance conductors, the main breaker, the panel bus bar and every circuit downstream.
  • Dimming Lights (electrical symptom): Dimming lights, called light flicker, are an electrical symptom where voltage drop across one or more circuits causes a measurable reduction in light intensity.
  • Flickering Lights (electrical symptom): Flickering lights, called light flickering, are an electrical symptom in which light intensity varies intermittently due to an unstable or interrupted voltage supply reaching the fixture.
  • Burning Smell (electrical panel): Burning smell (electrical panel), called overheating panel smell, is a type of Electrical Fault Symptom.
  • Outdated Electrical Panel: An outdated electrical panel, called a legacy electrical panel, is a type of electrical panel that has exceeded its recommended service life, lacks the amperage capacity to meet modern household demand, or relies on obsolete breaker technology that no longer meets current safety standards.
  • Federal Pacific Panel: Federal Pacific Panel, called an FPE panel, is a type of electrical panel manufactured and installed in residential homes from the 1950s through the 1980s, built around Stab-Lok circuit breakers, rated from 60 to 200 amperes, and fitted with 12 to 42 breaker slots depending on the model.
  • Zinsco Panel: A Zinsco panel, called a Zinsco electrical panel or Zinsco breaker box, is a type of electrical panel manufactured by the Zinsco company and installed widely in American homes from the 1950s through the mid-1970s.
  • Old Fuse Box: An old fuse box is a type of electrical panel.
  • Lack of Grounding (electrical system): Lack of grounding (electrical system), called an ungrounded electrical system, is a type of electrical deficiency in which a building's wiring has no continuous, low-resistance path to earth for fault current to follow, leaving outlets, panels and connected equipment without a safe discharge route during a short circuit, lightning surge, or insulation failure.
  • Meter Tampering: Meter tampering, called utility theft or illegal meter alteration, is the unauthorized alteration, bypassing or physical manipulation of an electric meter to make it under-register actual electricity consumption, including breaking tamper-evident seals, bypassing current transformers, modifying internal meter components, rerouting service conductors around the meter entirely and falsifying the recorded kilowatt-hour totals that a utility uses to calculate a customer's bill.
  • Utility Coordination for Meter Upgrade: Utility Coordination for Meter Upgrade, called Utility Scheduling, is the structured process of managing communication, documentation, permit applications, inspection scheduling and formal approvals between a property owner and the local utility company so a meter upgrade can proceed legally and without interruption to service.
  • Wiring Assessment: Wiring assessment, called an Electrical Wiring Evaluation, is a systematic evaluation of existing residential electrical wiring covering insulation condition, wire gauge, circuit continuity, grounding integrity, voltage ratings, current capacity, visual defect density and code compliance across every branch circuit in the home.
  • GFCI Protection: GFCI stands for Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter.
  • NEC Panel Upgrade Requirements: NEC Panel Upgrade Requirements, called service panel upgrade codes, are the rules set by the National Electrical Code governing how residential and commercial electrical service panels must be modified, replaced, or expanded to remain safe and legally compliant.
  • Florida Electrical Code: Florida Electrical Code, published as the Florida Building Code - Electrical, is a type of Electrical Code that sets the minimum safety requirements for the design, installation, inspection and maintenance of electrical systems in residential, commercial and industrial buildings throughout Florida, covering wiring methods, equipment standards, grounding, overcurrent protection, panel configurations and service entrance requirements.
  • Tampa Bay Electrical Permit: A Tampa Bay Electrical Permit, called an electrical work authorization, is an official document issued by a local governmental authority that grants legal approval to perform electrical work on a residential or commercial property in the Tampa Bay area, covering panel and meter upgrades, new wiring installations, outlet and switch work, generator connections, dock wiring and A/C or water heater replacement wiring.
  • Energy Efficiency Compliance: Energy Efficiency Compliance, called Energy Efficiency Certification, is a regulatory requirement confirming that a building's electrical and mechanical systems meet mandated performance standards.
  • Home Insurance Requirements (electrical upgrades): Home insurance requirements (electrical upgrades), called electrical policy stipulations, are a type of Insurance Underwriting Requirement that insurers impose on residential properties to confirm the electrical system meets minimum safety and code standards before issuing or renewing a homeowner's policy.
  • Home Electrical Capacity Increase: Home Electrical Capacity Increase, called an Amp Service Increase, is the replacement and expansion of a home's main electrical service.
  • Whole House Surge Protection Integration: Whole house surge protection integration, called Main Panel SPD Installation, is the installation of a Surge Protective Device directly into a home's main electrical panel to intercept voltage spikes before they reach any connected circuit, appliance, or wiring branch.
  • Load Balancing (Electrical): Load balancing (electrical), called phase balancing, is the process of evenly distributing electrical load across all phases and circuits in a building's power system.
  • UL Certification: UL Certification, known as Underwriters Laboratories Approval, is a type of Safety Certification that confirms a product, component or system has been independently tested and meets specific safety standards for use in North America, covering electrical panels, wiring devices, industrial control equipment, surge protection hardware and a wide range of other electrical components found in residential and commercial buildings.
